Tulsa's LGBTQIA+ Arts Scene Is the Best-Kept Secret in Oklahoma

A whole district built to hold it, and most of you have never heard of a single organization in it.

August 12, 2026 · Community Guide · 7 min read
🕒 7 min read · Published August 12, 2026

In this guide

  • Queer Art Tulsa Doesn't Advertise
  • The Center for Queer Prairie Studies
  • Circle Cinema and Twisted Arts
  • The Arts District Is the Gallery

Ask most people what Tulsa has going for it culturally and you'll get Gathering Place, maybe Philbrook, maybe a shrug. Ask them if there's a queer arts scene and you'll get a longer shrug. This is an insult, frankly, because Oklahoma is currently home to a full-time institution dedicated to queer prairie history, a film festival built entirely around LGBTQ+ storytelling, and a downtown arts district that has been quietly platforming queer artists for years without ever putting out a press release about it. Nobody told you because nobody had to. It was just here, doing the work, while the rest of the state made headlines for the opposite reason.

I am not going to let that stand. Here is the actual, verifiable, currently-operating queer arts infrastructure in Tulsa, Oklahoma, and where to find every piece of it.

Queer Art Tulsa Doesn't Advertise

Part of the problem is that Tulsa's queer arts institutions were built by artists, not marketers, which means they're excellent and nobody's heard of them. The other part is that this is Oklahoma, and institutions doing explicitly queer programming in a state with this legislature have learned to be quietly serious rather than loudly promotional. That doesn't make the work smaller. It makes it the kind of thing you have to actually go looking for, which, congratulations, is exactly what you're doing right now.

The Center for Queer Prairie Studies

This is the one that should stop you in your tracks. The Center for Queer Prairie Studies, known as CQPS, operates out of the Tulsa Artist Fellowship's Flagship public project space in downtown Tulsa. It is a working exhibition, archive, and event space dedicated entirely to 2SLGBTQ+ history and art in Oklahoma and the wider prairie region, curated by Tulsa Artist Fellow Karl Jones and built around the contributions of local queer artists and historical figures like Pat Gordon and playwright Lynn Riggs, whose own queerness Oklahoma spent decades not talking about.

Center for Queer Prairie Studies 112 N Boston Ave, Downtown Tulsa (Tulsa Artist Fellowship Flagship) Exhibitions, film screenings, lectures, fashion shows, and performance art, all centered on queer history and culture that Oklahoma's textbooks never mentioned. Check the calendar before you go, programming rotates and some events are one-night-only.

What makes CQPS worth a special trip, and not just a nice line in a guide, is that it doesn't treat queer Oklahoma history as a footnote to be apologized for. It treats it as the actual subject. That is rarer than it should be, in this state or any other, and it deserves your attendance, not just your bookmark.

Circle Cinema and Twisted Arts

If CQPS is the archive, Circle Cinema is the living room. Tulsa's nonprofit art-house theater in the Arts District has been the city's home for independent and international film for decades, and it has become, deliberately, the venue where Tulsa's queer film community actually gathers.

Circle Cinema 10 S Lewis Ave, Tulsa Tulsa's nonprofit indie movie house. Hosts free screenings with filmmaker Q&As in partnership with Black Queer Tulsa and the Center for Queer Prairie Studies, plus Twisted Arts' "Drag Me to the Movies" series, where a local drag artist performs before the film starts.
Twisted Arts / Twisted Fest Screens at Circle Cinema, 10 S Lewis Ave Tulsa's own LGBTQ+ film festival, founded in 2020, screening first-run queer feature films alongside 20 to 30 short films from local, national, and international filmmakers. This is not a college club running a projector in a classroom. This is a real festival with a real lineup, and it happens every single year.

Buy the Circle Cinema membership if you're planning to be in Tulsa more than a weekend. It funds a nonprofit doing genuinely rare work in a state that doesn't hand out arts funding to queer programming out of the goodness of its heart.

The Arts District Is the Gallery

Zoom out and you'll notice CQPS and Circle Cinema aren't isolated outposts, they're both sitting inside the Tulsa Arts District, the same several blocks that hold Club Majestic, the Tulsa Eagle, and half the queer-owned coffee shops in this city. That's not a coincidence. The Arts District has functioned as Tulsa's informal gayborhood for years precisely because the art, the nightlife, and the community organizing all grew up in the same six blocks at the same time. Walk it on a Saturday and you're not touring separate attractions, you're walking through one continuous queer cultural corridor that happens to have different buildings.

Philbrook Museum of Art, a few miles south in a converted 1920s villa, rounds out the picture with rotating exhibitions and a reliably welcoming staff, worth the drive when you want art with a garden attached instead of a bar next door. Between Philbrook, CQPS, and Circle Cinema, you have three genuinely different registers of queer cultural life in one metro area: institutional history, working film, and fine art. Most cities Tulsa's size have one, if they're lucky.

How to Actually Show Up

Knowing this exists and actually going are two different things, and the second one is the point. Check the CQPS calendar before a weekend trip, since exhibitions rotate and some programming is genuinely one night only. Buy a ticket to Twisted Fest when it runs, don't wait for someone to comp you one, festivals like this survive on the box office, not the applause. Follow Black Queer Tulsa and the Tulsa Artist Fellowship if you want the screenings and openings before they fill up. And if you're the kind of person who reads a paragraph like this and thinks "I could volunteer," email them. Small, serious institutions in a state like this one are always looking for hands that show up more than once.

None of this needed to exist here. Oklahoma didn't make it easy, and the legislature currently in charge of this state has made it actively harder. It exists anyway, funded, staffed, and running full calendars, because the people who built it decided Tulsa deserved it. The least you can do is walk in the door.
